**PoE BUDGET WARNING, read before specifying any switch on a camera job (ruled July 29, 2026, Wedgewood Marina quote session).** Port count and PoE budget are two different numbers and the UniFi naming hides it. The Standard 16 PoE above has sixteen ports but only eight of them deliver PoE and the total budget is 42W, which is roughly four cameras. The Lite 16 PoE is the same trap at 45W. The Lite 8 PoE has eight ports but only FOUR of them deliver PoE, against a 52W total budget at up to 30W per port, which in practice covers two access points and nothing else; it is a LAN flexibility and managed visibility switch, not a device that powers a room full of endpoints. Even the Standard 24 PoE is only 95W. Reaching for a switch because the port count looks sufficient is how a ten camera job arrives on site with a switch that cannot power it.

Size the switch by ADDING UP the camera draw, never by counting ports. Add each camera's maximum rated draw, and for any PoE+ device allocate the full 25.5W class rather than its typical draw, because the switch reserves by negotiated class and not by actual consumption. Then leave at least 30 percent headroom. Reference draws: G6 Bullet 9.9W (802.3af), AI LPR PoE+ (allocate 25.5W). Worked precedent, Wedgewood Marina: nine G6 Bullets at 9.9W plus one AI LPR at 25.5W is 114.6W, which rules out the 42W, 45W and 95W units and lands on the Pro Max 16 PoE at 180W.

Where a job does not need managed switching, the NICGIGA 18-Port 250W PoE+ switch in Section 3.4 is a legitimate substitute at a much lower price and powers cameras identically. Choose it deliberately, not by accident, and see the Section 8.7 note on what is lost without a Network controller.

**Buried run cable pricing rule (ruled July 20, 2026, amended July 27, 2026, Larry Lowenstein quote session):** the cable now carries a real catalog rate, and that rate applies to the cable EITHER WAY it is presented. There are two presentation methods and only the presentation differs, never the money.

DEFAULT, rolled into labour. On a standard buried networking run the cable is not shown as its own line item; its value is carried inside the installation labour line (for example "Trenching, buried cabling, concealed wiring & access point installation"). The quoting agent still computes the cable at the catalog rate above and folds that amount in, rather than absorbing it. Do not assume a run length; scope it from the site facts.

ON REQUEST, priced separately. Where Brenden says to price the cable out, or where the labour is already bundled into a single custom line and the cable would otherwise disappear inside it, the cable becomes its own line item at the catalog rate and the labour line is then labour only, described as the act of trenching, cabling and wiring rather than the wire itself.

The point of holding the rate in the catalog is that the margin survives either presentation. The failure mode this rule exists to prevent is a bundled labour line that quietly swallows several hundred dollars of cable at cost. If cable is ever quoted below the catalog rate, that is a deliberate discount and needs Brenden's word.

**Optical module selection rule (ruled July 27, 2026, Larry Lowenstein quote session):** the $38.99 module is a 1G part and caps a building-to-building link at 1 Gbps. **CRITICAL, TWO DIFFERENT $34.99 INJECTORS EXIST IN THIS CATALOG AND ONE OF THEM WILL DESTROY THE PHONE.** Section 3.8 lists the UniFi PoE+ Power Adapter (30W) at $34.99 and the 24V Passive PoE Injector (NanoStation) at $34.99. Identical price, similar shelf appearance, completely different electrically.

The PoE+ adapter is ACTIVE 802.3at at 48V and negotiates with the device before delivering power. That is the correct part.

The 24V passive injector pushes 24V down the cable continuously with no negotiation at all. Passive 24V is not interchangeable with 802.3af, at or bt, and connecting it to a device expecting active PoE can permanently damage it. Putting the NanoStation injector on a T88W Pro destroys a $599.99 phone.

Pick by part name and by the 802.3at marking on the unit, never by price and never by the bin it came out of. The same warning applies to any Yealink desk phone, not only the T88W Pro, since every phone in the lineup is 802.3af and none will tolerate passive 24V.

**Never specify PoE+ or PoE++ because you believe a desk phone REQUIRES it.** Every phone in the lineup is 802.3af and actual draw is under 6W on all of them. No desk phone anywhere in this catalog needs more than 802.3af.

The T88W Pro PoE+ injector above is NOT an exception to that. It is a sourcing decision rather than a power requirement: the phone still only needs 802.3af, and the injector is simply the 802.3at part McKee already stocks, used because the correct Yealink brick cannot be bought through our distributor. Do not read that line as evidence that the phone is a PoE+ device, and do not carry the reasoning across to any other phone.

**Do not let desk phones drive switch sizing.** Phone power is an adapter decision, not a network design decision. Never size up a PoE switch on account of desk phones. Where a switch is specified on a phone job it is for LAN capacity, managed visibility or access point power, and the phones are irrelevant to that choice.

Hardware standard: Yealink ONLY, no Grandstream. Residential and light commercial DECT: W70B base with W73H handsets; W59R for rugged or garage environments. Commercial desk phones: the four-tier T7X and T8X lineup above (T73U/W, T74U/W, T85W, T88W Pro). The T34W, T53W, T54W and T57W are retired and are not quoted on new work. Porting: LOA plus a recent bill required; partial ports use "Portion of Numbers" with a new BTN when other services remain on the account; never cancel the losing carrier until the port completes; watch for bundled internet on the losing account. Voicemail access is *98.

**Two or more numbers on ONE system: all phones ring on every number, one mailbox, one outbound caller ID (ruled August 6, 2026, Haliburton Vision Care Centre quote session; verified against the BrightPBX Call Handling, Users and Voicemail articles).** A client coming off multiple analog lines will ask to keep every number and will expect that to mean multiple lines again. It does not, and the answer is better than what they had. BrightPBX bills by USER, not by line, and with the trunk sliders at zero (see the Limits tab rule below) concurrent calls are unlimited, so the original reason for having two copper lines disappears entirely.

The build is three settings and no compromises:

Add EVERY ported number under SmartPBX then Main Number then Add from Spare Numbers. The panel accepts multiple DIDs at once, and all of them then share a single Incoming Call Handling configuration. There is no need for a second call flow, a second account or a second anything.

Point Incoming Call Handling at ONE ring group holding every user, with each user's delay bar at 0 seconds so all sets ring together. Because both numbers feed the same ring group, every phone rings on every number, and because there is one ring group there is ONE voicemail box rather than a mailbox per line, which is what clients almost always want and rarely know how to ask for.

Set the outbound caller ID at account level under SmartPBX then Dashboard then Caller ID, choosing the client's MAIN number. This is a single account-wide selection, so every user dials out showing the main number regardless of which number rang them. E911 is set against the same number and is mandatory before outbound calling works at all.

**EXTENSIONS ARE INTERNAL ONLY. An outside caller CANNOT dial an extension unless an auto attendant is answering the call (ruled August 6, 2026).** This gets promised by accident more than almost anything else on a phone quote, because "everyone gets an extension" sounds like it means the public can reach them by it. It does not. Something has to answer the call and collect the digits before an extension means anything to an outside caller, and that something is an auto attendant menu. Dial by extension is a feature OF the menu, not of extensions.

So there are exactly two ways an outside caller reaches one specific person:

An AUTO ATTENDANT answers first and offers the menu. This is a significant change to the client's experience, because callers now hit a recording instead of a person, and most small clients coming off analog lines explicitly do not want it. Never add one because it sounds capable. Add it only when the client asks for a menu.

A DEDICATED NUMBER pointed at that seat. The person gets their own DID at $4.99 per month, callers dial it directly, only that desk rings, and there is no menu anywhere. On a small site this is almost always the better answer, and it is cheaper than the client expects.

When describing what a client's CURRENT build does, say "another phone in the office can dial that desk directly" and not "callers can dial their extension". The first is true on every build. The second is true only where a menu exists.

**That is a rule about describing what is BUILT, not a ban on mentioning the auto attendant at all.** In an information section explaining what additional seats would open up, naming it is genuinely useful and clients ask about it later anyway. Present it as an available option with its tradeoff stated and clearly outside the quote, for example: "Once there is more than one seat you also have the option of a recorded greeting that invites a caller to dial an extension for the person they want. That would mean callers hear a recording rather than the phones ringing straight through, so we have not included it here, but it is available whenever you want it." Stating the tradeoff in the same breath is what keeps it informative rather than a sales line, and it means a client who does want a menu knows to ask.

Only reach for per-user dedicated DIDs or a second ring group where the client genuinely wants departments answered separately. Do not build that by default; it creates the multiple-mailbox problem the client was trying to escape.

**Seats versus numbers versus phones: scope this explicitly on every commercial deployment (ruled August 6, 2026).** These are three different counts and a client coming off analog lines will conflate all three, because on their old system a line WAS a number WAS a concurrent call. None of that holds here.

A NUMBER is a DID. It costs $1.75. Multiple numbers can point at the same ring group and the client cannot tell them apart on the way in.

A PHONE is a device. It costs nothing. Devices are assigned to a user in SmartPBX under Devices, using the wrench icon and the Assign To dropdown.

A SEAT is a user, which is an extension with a mailbox. It costs $5.95. Several phones can be assigned to one seat, and phones sharing a seat ring together and share one mailbox.

CONCURRENT CALLS are unlimited, because the trunk sliders sit at zero. Say this out loud to any client migrating off analog lines: the reason they had two lines was to take two calls at once, and that reason no longer exists. It is the single most valuable thing to tell them and they will not ask about it.

**Do not sell a client down to one seat purely to save them money.** One mailbox does NOT require one seat: a ring group across several seats, with its no-answer destination set to the main company voicemail box, still rings every phone together and still lands everything in one box. What extra seats actually buy is internal transfer between positions, direct reach to a person, and the ability to drop one phone out of the ring without touching the others. On any site where staff need to hand a call to somebody else in the building, that is a real workflow and it costs $5.95. Scope seats to how the business actually answers its phone, then confirm with the client before activation.

**Fax lines: the standing rule (ruled by Brenden McKee August 6, 2026, resolving the open item flagged July 2026).** Run this ladder in order and do not skip the first rung.

Ask what the fax is actually for. Most are dead lines nobody has used in years, and cancelling is the cheapest correct answer. Precedent: Haliburton Automotive was paying $33.62 a month for one Chris described as old.

Occasional use, such as parts sheets in and forms out, ports the fax number in as a BrightPBX fax box under Main Number then Main Faxbox Number, with an email address for inbound delivery and the Fax Manager app for outbound. The physical machine retires.

**Business critical faxing stays on copper and is NOT migrated, and no eFax option is offered on the quote at all.** This covers medical, dental, optometry, legal, insurance and anything with a compliance angle, prescriptions above all. BrightPBX states plainly in their own fax box article that faxing over VoIP has failure points outside their control, that they cannot guarantee 100% reliability of eFax, and that a client for whom faxing is critical should keep a separate POTS line. That is the vendor's own advice and repeating it to the client protects McKee. Do not put a fax box on the quote as an optional line either, because an option on a quote reads as a recommendation.

Porting mechanics when the fax stays behind: the voice numbers leave on a Portion of Numbers port and the fax number normally becomes the new BTN on the remaining account, since it is usually the only other dialable number there. Never cancel the losing carrier before the port completes.

**ASK FOR A COPY OF THE LOSING CARRIER'S BILL IN THE SAME MESSAGE THE QUOTE GOES OUT IN (ruled by Brenden McKee August 6, 2026).** The bill is not a nice to have, it is the document the whole port depends on, and asking for it at quote time means it is in hand when the client approves rather than becoming the thing that delays the cutover by two weeks.

The Letter of Authorization has to match the losing carrier's records EXACTLY: account holder name, billing telephone number, account number and service address. A mismatch on any single field rejects the port and restarts the clock. The bill is the only reliable source for those four; never take them from the client verbally, because what a client believes their account is called and what the carrier has on file are frequently different.

The bill also does three other jobs at no extra effort. It lists every number on the account, which is how the fax number surfaces along with any line nobody remembered they were paying for. It settles whether the lines are genuinely copper or are already being delivered over fibre through an ATA at the premises, which changes the fax reasoning above. And it shows what the client is actually paying today, which is the value conversation and is almost always a bigger number than they realise.

Client-facing wording that works, for the quote or the covering email: "To file the transfer we will need a recent copy of your bill from your current provider, since the paperwork has to match the account exactly as it appears there." Do not explain the LOA or the rejection risk to the client; it is our problem, not theirs.

**Recurring VoIP service is billed separately and is NEVER folded into the quote total or the payment splits (ruled by Brenden McKee August 6, 2026).** Monthly service is a separate recurring invoice on its own billing cycle and mixing it into a one-time installation total complicates both documents. **Configuration split, BrightPBX versus the W70B base (corrected July 29, 2026):** It is easy to place ring groups on the W70B local web interface. That is wrong and it sends a technician to the wrong device. The W70B has no ring group feature. What it has is Number Assignment, which is a different mechanism, and the two are easy to confuse because both decide which handsets make noise.

BrightPBX (SmartPBX) owns: SIP credentials, DID routing, main number and office hours strategy, incoming call handling, ring groups, ring timeout and the number of rings before voicemail, call queues, Virtual Receptionist / IVR, voicemail, caller ID, and E911. Anything to do with HOW LONG a call rings or WHICH EXTENSIONS ring is configured here, remotely, with no site visit.

The W70B local web interface owns: DECT handset registration and pairing to the base, Number Assignment, and device-level settings such as codecs and firmware.

**Number Assignment is not a ring group.** Under Account then Number Assignment on the base, the W70B presents a grid of SIP lines across and handset positions down, and assigns which lines each handset may receive on (Incoming Lines) and place calls on (Outgoing Lines). A handset only rings for the incoming lines assigned to it. This decides which HANDSETS ring for ONE SIP account on ONE base. A BrightPBX ring group decides which USERS or EXTENSIONS ring, potentially across different devices and locations. A single-extension DECT site with three handsets ringing together needs Number Assignment, not a ring group; a site where separate extensions must ring together needs a ring group in SmartPBX.

**Number Assignment is per handset, and it is set MANUALLY on every handset (ruled July 29, 2026, from the Haliburton Automotive incident).** Incoming and outgoing lines are assigned to each handset position individually. Provisioning the base does not do this for you and adding a handset does not inherit it from the handset beside it. Every handset must be set explicitly, and each one must be verified for BOTH directions before leaving site.

DIAGNOSTIC PRECEDENT, Haliburton Automotive, July 2026. Symptom: on a three-handset W70B, all three took inbound calls normally but two of the three could not place outbound calls. This was initially attributed to Starlink carrier-grade NAT. **CGNAT was never the cause.** The technician had assigned outgoing lines to handset 1 only, so the two remaining handsets had no outgoing line to dial on. Standing rule from this: a fault that affects SOME handsets on a base but not others is a Number Assignment fault, not a network fault. CGNAT, SIP ALG, firewall and one-way-audio causes are all account-wide or base-wide and cannot break two handsets while leaving a third working through the same WAN connection and the same SIP registration. Check the assignment grid FIRST and only escalate to network troubleshooting once every handset is confirmed assigned in both directions. Chasing the network on this pattern costs a site visit and finds nothing.

**Standard ring timeout, 36 seconds (ruled by Brenden McKee July 29, 2026, Haliburton Automotive).** Set Ring Timeout to 36 seconds on every user at provisioning, on new deployments and on existing accounts as they are touched. This is a McKee default, not a BrightPBX default; the platform ships at 20 seconds. Basis: North American ring cadence is a six second cycle, so 20 seconds delivers only 3.3 rings, which clients experience as the phone barely ringing before voicemail takes it. 36 seconds gives 6 rings. Do not go past roughly 42 seconds (7 rings): callers begin abandoning around 30 to 40 seconds, and a long timeout also delays voicemail, so an unanswered call yields neither a live pickup nor a message. Where a client still misses calls at 36 seconds the correct answer is a no-answer fallback to a mobile, not a longer timer. Precedent: Haliburton Automotive, McKee's first VoIP client, reported three rings as too few; 36 seconds was set and adopted as the standard the same day.

**Two standing gotchas on W70B installs.** First, the base assigns lines in a STAGGERED pattern by default, handset 1 to line 1, handset 2 to line 2, and so on. On a single-extension site that default leaves only handset 1 ringing, which presents as "the other phones do not ring and cannot call out." Fix it by assigning every handset to the one shared incoming line. Second, the W70B ships with Number of Active Handsets set to 5 against a maximum of 10 (Features then General Information); past five registered handsets the extras will not ring until that value is raised. Note also that a user can change their own incoming line assignment from the handset under Settings, Telephony, Incoming Lines, so an install that was correct on the day can be broken later by a client pressing buttons.

**Account-level Limits tab, standard configuration (ruled by Brenden McKee July 29, 2026, from the Haliburton Automotive account).** In the reseller portal, each customer account carries a Limits tab holding trunk caps, call restrictions and the credit balance controls. These are ACCOUNT-level settings and are separate from anything in SmartPBX or on the handsets. Set them at account creation, before the first test call.

TRUNKS ARE ALWAYS SET TO ZERO. The Twoway trunks, Inbound trunks and Outbound trunks sliders each set a cap on simultaneous calls, and zero means unlimited rather than none. Leave all three at zero on every account. Never set a trunk limit. A non-zero value silently caps concurrent calls and produces intermittent, hard-to-reproduce failures once the shop has more than one call up, which reads to the client as the system dropping calls at random.

Call restrictions, the "This account can place calls to" panel, are ticked for all destinations as standard: Canada, NA Tollfree, Emergency Dispatcher, N11 Special Services + 988, US Toll, USA and International.

**Toll-free (1-800) outbound failure, RESOLVED (July 29, 2026). Root cause corrected.** This was previously logged as a suspected missing rate in the BrightPBX rate deck, that is, a carrier-side problem. It was NOT. The fix is a single checkbox on the Limits tab under Manage credit balance: "Allow customers to make per-minute calls that go over the above limit, and bill them the per-minute cost of the call." With that box unticked, calls priced per minute rather than covered by the included plan are refused once they would exceed the per-minute credit balance, which is what killed toll-free dialling while ordinary local and long distance calls continued to work normally. Tick it on every account. Note that ticking NA Tollfree in the call restrictions panel is necessary but NOT sufficient on its own; both settings must be right. The Haliburton Automotive account carries a $50.00 per-minute credit balance with the overage box ticked, and toll-free dialling was confirmed working immediately afterward. Procedure for this is documented in the support portal (Section 3.12 portal reference); consult it before troubleshooting any call-restriction or credit-balance symptom.

**STANDING TRIAGE PRINCIPLE, ruled from the Haliburton Automotive deployment.** Two separate faults on this one site were each initially blamed on an external party, Starlink carrier-grade NAT for the outbound handset failure and the BrightPBX rate deck for the toll-free failure, and BOTH were McKee-side configuration that had simply been missed. Each cost significant diagnostic time. On any VoIP fault, exhaust our own configuration before attributing anything to the carrier, the ISP or the platform: the account Limits tab, the per-handset Number Assignment grid, the user settings in SmartPBX. A genuine carrier or transport fault degrades everything on the account at once. A fault that spares some handsets, some destinations or some call directions while leaving the rest working is ours until proven otherwise.

**VoIP commissioning checklist, run before leaving any site.** All three trunk sliders confirmed at zero. Call restrictions ticked for all destinations. Per-minute overage box ticked and credit balance set. Each handset placed a test outbound call. Each handset received a test inbound call. Every handset rings on the shared incoming line. Ring Timeout set to 36 seconds. Number of Active Handsets raised above 5 where more than five handsets are registered. Voicemail box reachable on *98 and the notification email correct. E911 address confirmed for the service address. Outbound caller ID displaying the client's main number. Toll-free (1-800) and long distance both dial out successfully, tested from every handset rather than from one.

Set a charge limit of 80 to 90 percent in the EcoFlow app on every permanent install to reduce long-term cell wear, and confirm AC output is set to stay on rather than timing out under a light load.

Runtime method for quoting. Divide capacity in watt-hours by the measured or estimated continuous load in watts, then apply 0.85 for inverter losses, and quote a RANGE, never a single figure. For Tier 2 lead-acid, derate to roughly 70 percent of nameplate watt-hours before applying that factor.

**Use TYPICAL draw, not max rated (ruled July 27, 2026).** Manufacturer "max power consumption" figures are worst case with every port lit and radios saturated, and sizing a battery against them understates runtime badly. Size against typical draw, then add 10 to 15 percent for PoE conversion loss on anything powered over Ethernet. During an outage the network is almost always quiet, which sits at the low end of typical. Reference loads, typical first and max rated second:

| Device | Typical draw | Max rated |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starlink Gen 2 Standard Actuated (dish, router and supply together) | 45-65W in light use, about 20W idle; add 15 to 30 percent in winter for the snow melt heater | 75W average under load |
| UniFi Cloud Gateway Fiber | 10-15W | 29.4W excluding PoE output |
| UniFi U7 class access point | 10-13W at the wall (independently measured at about 10W idle) | 21W |
| UniFi Flex 2.5G PoE switch | 10-14W | 17W excluding PoE output, on AC adapter input |

Worked precedent (Larry Lowenstein, July 27, 2026), 1024Wh unit, 870Wh usable at 0.85: Starlink plus gateway plus one access point computed at 66-95W gave 9 to 13 hours, and 74-115W in deep winter gave 8 to 11 hours; a Flex 2.5G switch plus three access points computed at 43-58W gave 15 to 20 hours. **Describe hardware by what it is LIKE TO USE, never by its spec sheet (ruled by Brenden McKee August 6, 2026).** A client is not buying sixteen SIP accounts or sixty programmable keys. They are buying something they will look at and touch several hundred times a week. A specification that does not change their daily experience does not belong in a client document at all, and listing it makes a quote read like a datasheet the client has to interpret rather than a recommendation they can act on.

Translate every spec into the experience it produces:

Screen size and resolution becomes whether a caller's name is easy to read from across the desk. An adjustable stand becomes readable whether you are sitting or standing at the counter. Backlit keys become easy to find in a dim room. Programmable keys become one touch dials for the people you ring most, never a number. Bluetooth becomes connects straight to a wireless headset so you can move while you talk. A cordless handset becomes answer at the desk and keep talking as you walk to the back. Noise suppression becomes keeps room noise off your side of the call.

Where a spec has NO experiential consequence for this particular client, leave it out entirely rather than including it for completeness. SIP account counts are the clearest example: a single seat practice will never care, and quoting the number invites a question that has no useful answer. Model numbers stay, since the client may want to look them up, but the prose around them is about use.

This applies to every category, not only phones. A camera's sensor size is what you can make out at the end of the driveway at night. A speaker's driver count is whether it fills the room.

**Separately, never present a BASELINE feature as though it were a selling point.** Some specifications are universal across a product category, so naming them differentiates nothing and makes the quote read like padding. Hearing aid compatible handsets are the clearest example: every business desk phone made has one, so writing it into a quote is claiming credit for meeting a standard rather than describing anything the client is choosing. The same goes for speakerphone, hold, transfer, caller ID and gigabit ports on a desk phone.

The test is simple. If EVERY option on the page has it, and every competitor's product has it too, it belongs in the quote only where the client specifically asked about it. Otherwise leave it out and spend the line on something that actually distinguishes the choice.

**And never describe a CAPABILITY in language that implies the accessory is included.** Writing that a phone "connects straight to a wireless headset" reads to a client as though a headset is coming with it. If no headset is priced on the quote, that sentence has sold something we are not delivering, and the client discovers it on install day.

Name the capability, then state the future option explicitly: "it has Bluetooth built in, so a wireless headset can be added later if anyone wants one." Same discipline applies across the board. A camera system "supports remote viewing on your phone" is fine; "watch your cameras from anywhere" implies an app subscription we have not quoted. ### 8.1 Honeywell Platform Rules

The 6160RF keypad contains a built-in wireless receiver (the integrated 5881ENH). Never spec a separate 5881ENH alongside a 6160RF; the standalone receiver exists only for builds without an RF keypad. The 6160RF is also what enables 5828 wireless keypads on a Vista system; the 5828 requires a 6160RF or 5883H transceiver plus its own R300-12033 always-on power transformer.

Vista 20P is the default and strongly preferred over ProSeries. Field experience with ProSeries includes power-outage recovery failures (systems fail to come back online without manual reset), general reliability concerns, and service complexity; backup generators only partially mitigate. When ProSeries is unavoidable, the PROTAKEOVER module lets a ProSeries panel receive legacy 5800-series wireless devices, and customers are told the limitations up front. 5800-series sensors pair with Vista panels (15P/20P/21iP/128BPT); ProSIX sensors pair with ProSeries only.

All new communicators are 4G/LTE (3G is sunset). The 7847i provides the IP path for sites without cellular service. ICF or concrete construction applies to EXTERIOR walls only; interior wireless sensors do not need a 5800RP repeater on that basis alone; add repeaters only when range testing demands it.

IP-monitored systems (7847i over Starlink or landline internet) REQUIRE battery backup on the security panel and the Starlink modem and network gear together, so monitoring communication survives power outages. The requirement is the protection, not a specific model: size it from the Section 3.19 tier table against the measured load and the runtime the site needs. A Tier 1 lithium unit is the default on IP-monitored, Starlink-dependent, remote or seasonally unoccupied sites, because a Tier 2 lead-acid UPS buys roughly 1 to 3 hours on that load while a 1024Wh lithium unit buys 8 to 21, and on an unoccupied property an outage that outlasts the backup means the system goes silent with nobody there to notice. Camera and network head-ends get the larger unit where post-outage recovery matters; single remote access points get the compact unit. Where the communication path spans two buildings, every building carrying part of that path needs its own backup.

Warranty statements: Honeywell/Resideo security hardware carries a 2-year manufacturer warranty. McKee provides a 1-year installation and workmanship warranty on labour. (Do not claim longer Resideo terms; the old "up to 5 years" marketing language is retired as unverifiable.)

### 8.2 Camera and Recorder Rules

UNV apps: UniEase (mobile) and EZ Station 3.0 (desktop). Never "UNV-Link". Luminys app: LumiViewer (replaces DMSS); recorders also expose a browser WebGUI. UNV warranty: 3 years on cameras and NVRs, 2 years on PTZ; Canadian administration by Petawise; unauthorized-reseller purchases can void it, which is the practical case against gray-market AliExpress Dahua alongside the firmware-region risk.

**UNV camera addressing after an NVR PoE port pairing (added July 30, 2026, from a garage camera call over a NanoStation bridge link).** A camera that has been pulled off an NVR PoE port and moved onto the site LAN reports a 172.16.0.x address. This is NORMAL and is not a fault. UNV recorders run their built-in PoE ports as a separate internal network, with the PoE NIC at 172.16.0.1 and a DHCP server enabled on it, so any camera that touches a PoE port is set into that range and keeps the address when it is moved elsewhere. There is nothing to diagnose. Re-address the camera and carry on.

**The address is not special. The only thing that matters is the subnet.** Set the camera to any free host address on the site's ACTUAL subnet, with a matching mask and gateway, and it comes straight up. Verify the third octet against the LAN the camera is going onto rather than assuming 192.168.1.x. A camera set to 192.168.1.20 on a 192.168.3.0/24 network is exactly as unreachable as one left on 172.16.0.x, and it is worse, because the address looks correct at a glance. That is the fault that cost the call this rule came from: the address was changed correctly in every respect except the third octet. Read the gateway off the router or off a working device on site before typing anything.

**Free, not random.** The address must be genuinely unused AND outside the DHCP pool of whatever router serves the site, or reserved on that router. An address taken from inside the pool works on the day and fails months later when the router leases it to something else.

**The camera password is no longer the factory password.** Plug and play does not only set an address. The recorder initializes the camera and sets a password on it during pairing, and that password is neither 123456 nor necessarily the one set on the NVR. A camera that refuses login after being pulled off a PoE port is showing this, not a fault; a reset button hold returns it to factory state and is the way out.

**Add it back on the native UNV protocol, never ONVIF.** On the recorder, change the channel's Add Mode from Plug-and-Play to IP Address and enter the camera's new address, leaving the protocol as UNV. ONVIF carries basic compatibility only and loses smart event support, so adding a UNV camera to a UNV recorder over ONVIF quietly costs SMD on that channel.

PTZ power: the IPC6434LR draws roughly 23W (PoE+ 802.3at); NVR built-in PoE budgets rarely carry PTZ loads, so PTZ builds always include the NICGIGA external PoE+ switch. Every PTZ mounts through the TR-UF45-F-IN adapter plate onto its bracket.

Luminys recorder AI limits (R-series class): SMD Plus on up to 8 channels, perimeter protection on 4, face detection on 2; enabling AcuPick disables recorder-side analytics; camera-side SMD remains available on all channels regardless.

Frame TVs accept ONLY Samsung's proprietary flush wall mount (included with the TV); third-party mounts do not fit. The StrongBox recessed box houses the One Connect unit.

TSSA compliance applies to fuel-station camera installations (Clauses 5.1.2(b) and 5.2.6): camera and wiring placement within fuel dispensing zones must respect hazardous-location boundaries.

### 8.3 Network and Transmission Rules

Copper Ethernet is limited to 328 ft (100 m); beyond that, or through obstructions, use OS2 single-mode fiber. The UniFi Building Bridge requires clear line of sight (rated to 5 km). NanoStation bridges always deploy as a pair with 24V passive injectors. A NanoStation or Building Bridge link is a transparent layer 2 bridge, so the two buildings sit on ONE subnet in ONE broadcast domain: a camera or any other device at the far end is on the main LAN in every sense that matters, and is discovered and addressed exactly as though it were plugged in at the head end. Armoured fiber: bond the steel armour at one end only; all-dielectric cable avoids grounding entirely. Guest network isolation and subnet restrictions are configured through UniFi network access controls.

Cloud Gateway Fiber (UCG-Fiber) port map, confirmed July 27, 2026. Two SFP+ 10G ports: one is WAN, the SECOND IS A DEDICATED LAN PORT, which is what lets a fiber run to another building originate at the gateway itself with no separate fiber switch. One 10G RJ45 WAN port, which negotiates 1G and 2.5G and takes a Starlink Ethernet Adapter directly. Four 2.5GbE LAN ports, with port 4 delivering PoE+ up to 30W, enough for a U7 Pro or U7 Pro Max without a separate injector. The 54V power adapter ships in the box, unlike the Flex 2.5G PoE switch, whose adapter is a separate line item. Field note: a Fluke MicroScanner reads that PoE port as a 6.5W Class 2 device, which is a negotiation reporting quirk and not the delivered power; U7 Pro and U7 Pro Max are confirmed running from it. Do not add an injector on the strength of that reading. Standard remote-site pairing: Cloud Gateway Fiber at the head end feeding a Flex 2.5G PoE switch at the far building over 10G single-mode fiber, which gives a 2.5 Gbps or better backbone end to end.

### 8.5 Starlink Technical

Performance: 50-350 Mbps down, 10-30 Mbps up, 25-50 ms latency, unlimited data with fair use. Clear sky view above 25 degrees elevation required. Integration paths: standalone (Starlink router), professional (bypass mode into UniFi with VLANs and managed WiFi), hybrid (Starlink primary with cellular failover). The Gen 3 J-Arm kit includes the pipe adapter; customers order the Standard Kit only. Residential Max is the recommended plan for fixed and business installs; the Mini is not a fixed-primary solution.

Gen 2 dish integration. Bypass mode, set in the Starlink app under Settings then Advanced, disables the Starlink WiFi and is the correct configuration when UniFi is providing the network; reverting requires a factory reset. Gen 3 and later kits have Ethernet built in and need no adapter. Gen 2 splice-and-extend cabling method (ruled July 27, 2026, Larry Lowenstein quote session). Where the dish must sit well away from the equipment location, do not hunt for a longer proprietary Gen 2 cable. Direct burial Cat6 is a heavier gauge than the factory Starlink cable and is rated for burial, which the factory cable is not. The second run doubles as a dedicated spare, so a later move to a newer dish generation is a swap at the mast and a reconnection inside rather than a second trench. Cable is priced per the Section 3.10 buried run cable pricing rule, either folded into the labour line or shown as its own line item, and the splice kit is always its own line item.

### 8.7 UniFi Protect Platform Rules

Added July 29, 2026 (Wedgewood Marina quote session). Pricing for this platform lives in Section 3.21 and Section 3.8. Read this section before quoting any UniFi Protect camera system. The failure modes below are all ones that cost either a site visit or a client conversation.

**License Plate Recognition: which cameras do it, and the AI Key question.** Per Ubiquiti's own AI Detections capability table, License Plate Recognition is NATIVE on the G6 series and the AI series, excluding 360 models, and searching recordings by plate is done in the Find Anything tab. Neither carries an AI Key requirement. A G6 camera plus any Protect console gives plate reads, the plate log and plate search with no accessory of any kind.

**The AI Key does NOT add License Plate Recognition, and this was got wrong once already.** The AI Key is a local AI co-processor that adds the DESCRIPTIVE layer: clothing type and colour, face enhancement, gender and accessory detection, speech to text, AI enhanced alarms, advanced AI summaries and natural language search. That is the "person in a red jacket by the shop door" capability. It is not the plate layer. Quote an AI Key only when a client specifically wants natural language search across their footage, and price it as its own optional line rather than folding it into a plate capture job.

**The AI Port is the part that adds LPR to older and third-party cameras.** On G5 and G4 series, License Plate Recognition, Face Recognition and vehicle type and colour all require an AI Port. The AI Port also brings Protect AI features to third-party ONVIF cameras, UNV included, at one camera per port. That is a genuine path to adding plate capture to an existing UNV camera, but at one camera per port the cost stacks quickly, and it must be bench tested against a UNV camera before it appears on any quote. Standing summary: G6 and AI series need nothing, G5/G4 and ONVIF need an AI Port, and the AI Key is a separate product for a separate purpose.

**A standalone UNVR does NOT run the UniFi Network application. This is the answer to the gateway question.** The UNVR and UNVR Pro are Protect-only consoles; they also host Access and InnerSpace, but Network is reserved for the Cloud Gateway range. The consequences on a quote are precise:

The cameras and Protect need NO gateway. The UNVR is its own UniFi OS console, takes a DHCP address from whatever router is already on site, and delivers full local recording plus remote access through unifi.ui.com and the Protect app. On a camera-only job the correct answer to "do we need a gateway" is no.

A UniFi SWITCH on that same site cannot be adopted or managed, because there is no Network controller to adopt it. It passes traffic and delivers PoE, but it will not appear in any UniFi interface. If a client is told they are getting a managed UniFi system and the switch is invisible, that is a broken promise. Either add a Cloud Gateway so the switch is managed, or use the NICGIGA switch from Section 3.4 and do not describe the switching as managed.

Recommend a gateway only where the client actually wants their NETWORK managed, and be explicit that it becomes their router. On a camera-only job at a site with a working ISP router, adding a gateway is scope creep and puts McKee on the hook for their internet. Precedent: Wedgewood Marina was quoted UNVR with no gateway on exactly this reasoning.

**Storage, RAID and the non-hot-add trap.** UniFi applies RAID by drive count, not by choice: one drive runs with no RAID at full capacity, two drives are RAID 1, and three or more are RAID 5 under Basic Protection. Advanced Protection takes four or more to RAID 6. A single drive is a legitimate configuration where the client does not want redundancy.

THE UNVR IS NOT A HOT-ADD SYSTEM. Adding a drive after the fact reinitializes the array and destroys existing footage. The drive count must therefore be decided on install day and confirmed with the client in writing, because "we can add another drive later" is only true at the cost of everything recorded so far. Where there is any prospect of wanting more retention in year two, put the drives in now.

Retention sizing method. Ubiquiti's own reference is 24TB usable covering fifteen 4K cameras for 30 days, which works out to roughly 0.053TB per 4K camera per day. Multiply by camera count for a daily figure, then divide usable capacity by it. Worked precedent, Wedgewood Marina: ten 4K cameras burn about 0.53TB per day, so 10TB gives roughly 19 days of continuous recording and materially longer on motion based retention. Quote retention as a RANGE and as an estimate, never as a single committed figure.

**PoE sizing.** See the PoE budget warning in Section 3.8. The short version is that UniFi port counts and PoE budgets are unrelated numbers and the Standard 16 PoE at 42W cannot run a camera system.

**What is and is not in the cloud, stated correctly.** Protect DOES log plates. The camera boxes the vehicle, reads the plate, and stores the plate string with a confidence percentage as a timestamped detection linked to the clip, searchable by plate, and usable as an Alarm Manager trigger for notifications, lighting, PTZ movement or a door unlock through UniFi Access. Detections and footage can also be archived automatically to a NAS or to a cloud service such as Google Drive, OneDrive or Dropbox, configured under Settings then Off-Site Archiving.

What Protect does NOT provide is a hosted plate database independent of the recorder. Ubiquiti runs no plate service on the client's behalf. The system of record is the console, so if a recorder is stolen and off-site archiving was never configured, the plate history goes with it. Configure off-site archiving on every install at a remote, seasonal or unoccupied site; it is a setup step McKee controls, not a missing feature. Where a client genuinely wants a hosted cloud plate database with independent alerting, UniFi is the wrong platform and the honest answer is to say so rather than stretch the description.

**AI LPR mounting geometry, confirm before quoting.** Maximum 5 m mounting height and under 25 degrees from the direction of travel both horizontally and vertically, relaxing to 15 m and 30 degrees for slow or stopped traffic. In night mode the camera's glare cut filter means it sees plates and headlights and nothing else, so it NEVER doubles as a general scene camera; where the client also wants to see the vehicle and driver, a second camera covers that view. Walk the entrance and confirm the geometry works before the camera reaches a quote, because a plate camera mounted too high or too far off axis reads nothing at night and the client will measure that.

**Protect adoption notes.** Cameras must sit on the same VLAN as the Protect host or the host has to be specified manually on each camera at its web interface using default credentials. Adoption uses TCP 7444, 7550 and 7442 and UDP 10001. A camera cannot be adopted by two consoles at once, and moving one between consoles requires removing it from the first.

### 8.8 Analog to IP Camera Retrofit Over Existing Coax

Added July 30, 2026 (Haliburton Timber Mart quote session). Pricing lives in Section 3.8 and Section 3.3; the build template is 7.17. Read this section before quoting a retrofit extender on any job.

**The transmitter requires PoE++ and an NVR cannot feed it.** Input is 802.3bt, 50V DC at 1.2A. The built-in PoE ports on a UNV or Luminys recorder are af/at and will NOT power it. This is verified against Ubiquiti's own tech specs page, two independent distributor listings and a field report, and it is the single most expensive thing to get wrong on this platform because it is discovered on site with the old system already down. Every retrofit job therefore carries one 60W PoE++ adapter per link plus a plain non-PoE gigabit switch to feed them, and the recorder's own PoE ports go unused for these cameras.

**Feed method ruling: individual adapters, never a PoE++ switch.** A switch reserves PoE by negotiated class rather than by actual draw, so six bt devices can lay claim to well past a 180W budget even though the real load is a fraction of that. Individual adapters give each link a dedicated 60W supply with no budget contention, and on the Timber Mart job they came in roughly $360 below the Pro Max 16 PoE. Do not reach for a switch because it looks tidier.

**Receiver output** is PoE+, 48V DC at 0.6A, which is ample for a 4MP dome and leaves headroom.

**Coax outperforms twisted pair on power, so use the coax.** At 100 m RG-6 carries 20W where UTP 24AWG carries 13W. Legacy siamese CCTV cable gives us both a coaxial conductor and a two-wire power pair, so run the link on the coax through the supplied BNC adapters and leave the two-wire pair in place as a documented spare. That spare is worth saying out loud to the client.

**One camera per link.** Each link is 10/100 MbE over Homeplug AV at roughly 85 Mbps. That is comfortable for 4MP and is a large part of why 4MP is the right specification on a retrofit. Check the bitrate before putting a high bitrate 4K camera on a link, and never plan two cameras behind one receiver.

**OPERATING TEMPERATURE, -10 to +40 C. This is a hard design constraint in this region, not a footnote.** Haliburton routinely sits fifteen to twenty degrees below that floor in January, so a receiver module mounted outdoors is out of rating for a large part of every year.

Assume from the outset that the cameras are outside, because on a retrofit they almost always are. That does NOT mean the receiver goes outside with them.

STANDARD McKEE METHOD, ruled July 30, 2026 from the Haliburton Timber Mart build. Transmitters group at the head end, in the utility or equipment room beside the recorder, which is where the existing coaxial runs and their power already terminate on an analog system. Receivers mount INSIDE THE WALL CAVITY behind each camera, reached by cutting the existing camera penetration larger. The module is concealed, the camera end remains a straight swap, and the electronics stay in rating.

Three conditions make the cavity method work, and all three are settled on install day.

The module sits on the WARM SIDE of the insulation, in the stud bay between the interior finish and the batt. There it runs within a few degrees of room temperature. Outboard of the insulation it is barely better than an outdoor box.

The camera penetration is SEALED around the tail. That opening passes straight through to outside with the module sitting at it, so an unsealed hole puts a cold air path onto the module and brings condensation with it where warm interior air meets the cold edge. Sealing it is the difference between a warm cavity and a draft.

The camera base COVERS the opening. Buried in a cavity the module's diagnostic LEDs are invisible, so a link negotiating badly gives no visible sign. Where the dome base covers the hole, pulling the camera is a four screw job and reaches the module directly. Confirm the base actually covers the opening being cut before cutting it.

NO JUNCTION BOXES on this build. The standing one-per-camera rule in Section 5.4 does not apply where the receiver and every connection sit inside the wall cavity; the box would house nothing.

Never quote a retrofit that leaves receiver modules in an outdoor junction box in this climate. The entire premise of an analog to IP retrofit is that the client is buying stability, and hanging out-of-rating electronics at every camera position undoes the thing being sold. If a position genuinely has no practical interior break point, raise it with Brenden and solve that camera differently rather than accepting an outdoor module by default. Cold weather failures land in January, at the worst possible time, on a system installed to stop exactly that kind of call.

**Old coax is often RG59, not RG6.** Ubiquiti's distance and power table is built on RG-6. RG59 has higher loss and gives less headroom on both power and reach. It is generally acceptable at normal in-building distances, but identify the cable type on the site walk rather than assuming, and treat a long RG59 run as the one most likely to disappoint.

**Why the PoE++ requirement feels wrong to anyone who has done a coax retrofit before.** Ethernet-over-coax converters from LINOVISION, Veracity, Altronix and NVT have been trade standard for years and they run directly off an NVR's PoE or PoE+ port. That experience does not transfer. The Ubiquiti part is the odd one out, it is filed by Ubiquiti under Door Access accessories, and it is specifically a bt-in device. If anyone recalls doing this without PoE++, they are almost certainly recalling one of the other converters. Verify against the Section 3.8 entry rather than against memory.
